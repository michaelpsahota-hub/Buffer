Title: The guinea pig experiment that found lithium

In the late 1940s, Melbourne psychiatrist John Cade injected his patients' urine into guinea pigs. When he gave them lithium on its own, the animals went calm, and he wondered if it could calm mania in people.

The twist: historians now think that calm was probably early lithium poisoning. Right idea, wrong reason.

===== Short 2 · short2_the_melbourne_blood_test_that_saved_lithium.mp4

Title: The Melbourne blood test that saved lithium

Lithium was nearly abandoned over fears about toxicity. The rescue started in Melbourne.

At the University of Melbourne, scientist Edward Trautner realised lithium could be measured in the blood with a new machine, the flame photometer. With psychiatrist Charles Noack, he treated patients while checking their blood levels and watching closely for side effects. They published in 1951. Lithium didn't need to be abandoned. It needed to be measured.

If you take lithium: don't change your medication without talking to your prescriber.

===== Short 3 · short3_why_the_us_waited_more_than_20_years_for_lithium.mp4

Title: Why the US waited 20+ years for lithium

Lithium is a simple salt. Nobody could patent it, so no drug company had much reason to push for it.

The US finally approved lithium for mania in 1970, more than 20 years after John Cade's 1949 paper. By then, 49 other countries already had. The US was the 50th.
